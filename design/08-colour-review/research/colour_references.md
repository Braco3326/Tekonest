# Colour stereotypes, clichés and grounded references for a Paris culturally-mixed nightlife + sound-training brand

## 1. Documented critiques of orientalist / exoticising colour and visual codes (SWANA and African/Afro-diasporic)

### Takeaway
There is good sourced critique of generic "African design" framing and of the colonial history of wax print, and trade-press critique of Ramadan/Arab marketing clichés (lanterns, crescents, oud, "dusk-blue lighting"). I found no design-press source that names the exact "gold + turquoise + deep red Arabian nights" palette or a generic red-gold-green palette as a cliché. That point is a reasonable inference, not a cited finding.

### Cited Findings
**SWANA / Arab**
- AGBI opinion column by Austyn Allison (has covered Middle East advertising since 2007): it lists the recurring Ramadan ad clichés as "crescent moons, lanterns, and dates", "gentle oud music and dusk-blue lighting", a child's first fast, and the grandfather at "the heaped iftar table". Allison argues the clichés persist because they are emotional shorthand. He warns that AI will reproduce them and recommends evolution through humour and irreverence rather than giving them up. Gold is not named. — [AGBI, "Lamenting Ramadan cliches is the biggest cliche of all" (2025)](https://www.agbi.com/opinion/media/2025/03/lamenting-ramadan-cliches-is-the-biggest-cliche-of-all/)
- Campaign Asia (Surekha Ragavan) describes regional Ramadan/Eid marketing as "often made up of damaging tropes and lazy narratives". The body text was not retrieved, so no specific visual tropes can be cited from it. — [Campaign Asia](https://www.campaignasia.com/article/how-can-marketers-make-better-ramadan-ads/gzkkn2t0mvixx2fuqmf9ywkqbr)
- Huda Smitshuijzen AbiFarès (founder of the Khatt Foundation) framed her 2008 symposium talk as "asserting the arab identity by providing it with a visible platform". The aim was to give Arab designers an active role through contemporary Arabic typography (the "typographic matchmaking" project), not through ornament. This page does not discuss colour or orientalism explicitly. — [Typographische Gesellschaft Austria 2008 abstract](https://typographischegesellschaft.at/a_symposium/s_2008/abifares.html)
- The Henry Ford museum has an article on 19th-century Western decorative arts as "perceptions of the Middle East", the historical root of the Victorian "oriental" décor vocabulary. It was not fetched, so its content is unverified. — [The Henry Ford](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/perceptions-of-the-middle-east-in-19th-century-decorative-arts)

**African / Afro-diasporic**
- africa.com (SG Editor, 16 May 2021, via House Beautiful) argues that the umbrella term "African design" shrinks many aesthetics into Western stereotypes. It quotes a 2013 HuffPost description built on "Primitivism", wildlife, "earthy hues" and "the colour orange", with no specific country named. It says the term reduces a range across 46 sub-Saharan countries to a few export-driven tourist-art motifs, mostly from South Africa, Ghana and Nigeria. It also notes that the Sahel brings in Persian, Arab and Bedouin influences. — [africa.com](https://africa.com/top-10-news/african-decor-and-design-why-this-term-is-problematic)
- Wax print history (BBC feature by Clare Spencer, republished by MyJoyOnline on 26 June 2020):
  - The technique derives from Javanese batik, which the Dutch mechanised.
  - Ebenezer Brown Fleming brought the first Dutch industrial batiks to the Gold Coast in 1893.
  - Vlisco (Dutch) owns GTP.
  - Designer Nana Kwame Adusei: "The wax print companies have been making money for 170 years and the money doesn't come back to us."
  - Others, such as Amma Aboagye, argue the cloth has become African through use. Researcher Helen Elands points out that most buyers are African and gives the 1920s "staff of kingship" design as an example of consumer agency.

  — [MyJoyOnline/BBC](https://www.myjoyonline.com/wax-print-africas-pride-or-colonial-legacy/)

### Inferences
- "Ornament as cultural signifier" is the risk: lanterns, crescents, arabesque or mosaic fills, and wax-print or kente patterns used as background decoration. Cited sources tie these motifs to cliché or tourist/export framing, so the same risk very likely carries over to their typical palettes: gold/turquoise/jewel tones and earth/orange "safari" tones.
- Wax print is contested rather than simply "inauthentic". A brand should not use it as generic "Afro" decoration. Commissioning or crediting artists, as with the Ghanaian "staff of kingship" example, is a more defensible route.
- The SWANA/Afro programming spans many traditions. A single "cultural" accent colour standing in for all of them repeats the flattening that africa.com criticises.

### Gaps
- No source found from the Khatt Foundation, Eye on Design or It's Nice That that names the "gold + turquoise + deep red" orientalist palette specifically. Academic visual-orientalism papers turned up in search (e.g. academia.edu/33684768, Courtauld "Drawing on Arabian Nights") were not verified.
- No sourced critique found of pan-African red-gold-green used as generic "Afro" branding.
- Quartz's "Not African Enough" piece (Kenyan fashion designers) appeared in search but was not fetched: [qz.com](https://qz.com/africa/1087269/not-african-enough-kenyan-fashion-designers-push-back-in-a-new-book/).

## 2. Colour meanings that differ across the relevant cultures

### Takeaway
Green has documented Islamic associations (Qur'anic paradise garments, the Prophet's garments, flags). Those associations are not universal, and dynastic colours historically varied (Abbasid black, Fatimid white). Treat this as awareness, not a rulebook.

### Cited Findings
- Qur'an 18:31 describes green silk garments in Paradise. A hadith in Sunan Abu Dawood (Abu Rimthah) says Muhammad wore two green garments. Al-Khidr is "the Green One". The current Green Dome in Medina dates from 1818. — [Wikipedia, Green in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_in_Islam)
- Historically the Abbasid dynastic colour was black and the Fatimid colour was white. Green was adopted only briefly by al-Ma'mun in 817 (Wikipedia tags this claim as dubious). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_in_Islam)
- Green on flags is not always Islamic. Bangladesh, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan use it for other reasons. Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Mali and Senegal use it as a pan-African colour. Saudi Arabia and Pakistan use it for Islam. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_in_Islam)

### Inferences
- Avoid giving green a "sacred" or "Islamic" role in a nightlife context, for example on an alcohol-adjacent bar or club page. Using green as a functional UI colour is not inherently problematic.

### Gaps
- Mourning colours (white, black, other) across the Maghreb, Levant and West Africa: search results (e.g. altima-sfi.com, ara.cat "white in Asia, orange in Middle East") were low-authority or unverified and are not cited. Red symbolism was also not verified. Ethnographic sources are needed before making any claim.

## 3. Nightlife / club-culture visual clichés called out by design writers

### Takeaway
Eye on Design documents "acid graphics" (saturated palettes heavy on neon green, warped type, liquid metal), "terminal green" and liquid chrome as dominant 2019-era trends. Practitioners in the same coverage call maximalist acid style "lazy" and "maxed out". Creative Review notes that club visual codes have been co-opted by brands, even the CIA, which drains them of meaning.

### Cited Findings
- Emily Gosling, "'Acid Graphics' Are the New Psychedelia—With a Heady Dose of Cynicism", Eye on Design, 5 Sep 2019:
  - Defines the style as bright saturated palettes "with a lot of neon green", op-art, warped or reversed type and liquid-metal lettering, with nightclub posters as its "natural home".
  - Quotes "That 'very trippy, lots of typefaces' thing is maxed out" and calls maximalism potentially "lazy" and "shallow" (Hoppmann).

  — [Eye on Design](https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/acid-graphics-are-the-new-psychedelia-with-a-heady-dose-of-cynicism/)
- Liz Stinson, "The Year in Design: 5 Trends that Defined 2019", Eye on Design, 24 Dec 2019. The five trends are acid graphics, liquid metal ("already reached peak popularity"), "terminal green" neon, Alegria illustration and blobby kinetic identities. — [Eye on Design](https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/the-year-in-design-5-trends-that-defined-2019/)
- Megan Williams, "Have club graphics been co-opted?", Creative Review, 14 Jan 2021. It covers the CIA's 2021 rebrand, which used chunky outlined type (compared to Crack Magazine), 3D line patterns, LED-like effects and Unknown Pleasures-style topography. Ian Anderson (The Designers Republic) criticises it. — [Creative Review](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/clubs-records-brands-graphic-design/)

### Inferences
- Acid/neon green on black, liquid chrome, and warped maximalist type are now generic "club" signifiers, used even by intelligence agencies. They will not differentiate the brand.

### Gaps
- No authoritative design-press source was retrieved that specifically calls purple/pink synthwave or vaporwave gradients a nightlife cliché. A Mixmag Asia "vaporwave/acidgrafix/cybersigilism" feature and a 2026 Binus critique of "neo-rave chrome" appeared in search but were not verified.

## 4. Grounded trade-native colour references (gels, cables, Paris light, Risograph)

### Takeaway
Lee 162 Bastard Amber and 117 Steel Blue are verified gel names with official descriptions, but no official hex values exist. Audio multicore looms commonly use the resistor colour code for channel IDs, while cable-length tape colours have no standard. Paris has been replacing its old sodium lighting with warm LED (2,700 K on the Rue de Rivoli) since a 2011 performance contract, with a further 70,000 LED fixtures planned in a 2021 contract. RISO ink names are available from print shops, but the official RISO list was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
**Theatre gels**
- LEE 162 Bastard Amber: "A warm white / warm wash. Good lamplight effect." LEE cross-references it to Zircon 808 Warm Amber 6. — [LEE Filters colour details](https://www.leefilters.com/lighting/colour-details.html#117)
- LEE 117 Steel Blue: "Good for cool washes. Adds a pale green tint. Great for emulating icy weather on stage." — [LEE Filters](https://www.leefilters.com/lighting/colour-details.html#117)
- 162 transmission is 77.7%, "Excellent for natural skin tones" (retailer page, which treats the Rosco E-Colour+ 162 equivalent the same way). No hex value is given; the page recommends swatch books for exact matching. — [Stage Depot](https://stagedepot.co.uk/node/1976)
- The LEE page gives no numeric colour values, so any hex value for these gels is an approximation.

**Cable colour coding**
- Hinton Instruments XLR looms use resistor-code colours for channels: 1 brown, 2 red, 3 orange, 4 yellow, 5 green, 6 blue, 7 violet, 8 grey. — [Hinton Instruments](https://hinton-instruments.co.uk/paprod/cables/analogue/xlrloom/hd/4c.htm)
- For cable lengths (DMX), practitioners on the Blue Room forum say "there definitely isn't a standard!!" and use coloured LX/gaffer tape with house-specific keys. — [Blue Room forum](https://www.blue-room.org.uk/topic/38346-dmx-cable-colour-coding/)
- Amphenol sells an "AX MARK" colour-coding sleeve system for XLR. It appeared in search but was not fetched. — [Mouser](https://www.mouser.se/amphenol-audio-ax-mark-color-coding-sleeve-system)

**Paris public lighting**
- Paris has about 345,000 light sources across signalling, road, green-space lighting and illuminations.
- A 10-year energy-performance contract started in 2011. By the end of 2013, more than 8,000 luminaires had been replaced and 55 junctions relit with LED.
- The target was −30% lighting consumption by 2020 against a 2004 baseline.
- High-pressure sodium is mentioned (with LED) for bridge illumination at Pont d'Arcole.

  — [Plan Climat Paris, July 2015 (Energy Cities PDF)](https://energy-cities.eu/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Paris_eclairage_public_2015_fr.pdf)
- Ville de Paris press release, 4 Nov 2021:
  - New 10-year, €704M contract with CIELIS (Citelum + Eiffage).
  - 70,000 LED fixtures and more than 12,000 posts replaced.
  - About −30% energy per year and 240 GWh saved over the contract.
  - Variable colour temperature in sensitive zones (Seine banks, canals, parks).

  — [paris.fr press PDF](https://cdn.paris.fr/presse/2021/11/04/04cd6e9c778131c794e09cbce78172f1.pdf)
- Rue de Rivoli: 120 W sodium lamps (Schréder calls them "white sodium") were replaced with 2,700 K warm-white LEDs averaging 19 W, linked to COP21 (2015). — [Schréder project page](https://ae.schreder.com/en/projects/energy-efficient-led-optics-preserve-heritage-rue-de-rivoli-and-cuts-energy-impressive)

**Risograph inks**
- Moniker Press (a Canadian riso shop) lists Black, Bright Red, Medium Blue, Green, Yellow, Purple, Orchid, Fl(uorescent) Pink, Fl Orange, Aqua (custom), Cornflower, Teal, Light Grey, Mahogany, Orange, Flat Gold and Light Lime (out of stock). No hex values are given. — [Moniker Press](https://www.monikerpress.ca/colours/)
- The UT Austin print wiki points to stencil.wiki/colors as its source for riso colours and hex values. stencil.wiki could not be fetched. — [UT wiki](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/utprint/pages/53086355)
- RISO publishes a "Communication Color" blog page (not fetched). — [us.riso.com](https://us.riso.com/resources/blog/communication-color/)

### Inferences
- The "sodium orange → 2,700 K LED" shift gives a genuinely Paris-specific, time-stamped light story: warm, not neon. A Bastard Amber / Steel Blue pair maps onto that stage-light vocabulary.
- Using the resistor-code sequence for categories would be trade-authentic for sound technicians. It is an 8–10 hue rainbow, though, which runs into the accessibility limits in section 5.
- A two-ink riso system (e.g. a warm red/orange plus a blue) would be credible. Ink names should be checked against RISO's own list before use.

### Gaps
- Official RISO ink list and official Rosco/LEE spectral or hex data were not retrieved. Hex values for gels and inks must be marked approximate.
- No exact date was found for when Paris finished phasing out sodium, and the overall share of sodium versus LED is unknown.
- No formal audio-industry standard for cable-length colours was found; the sources say none exists.

## 5. Accessibility research on colour coding for navigation

### Takeaway
WCAG 1.4.1 forbids colour as the only means of conveying information, so category colours need a second cue (label, number, icon, or a lightness difference of at least 3:1). Established categorical palettes top out around 8 colours (Okabe–Ito, most ColorBrewer sets), with 12 as the maximum in ColorBrewer's Paired and Set3.

### Cited Findings
- WCAG SC 1.4.1: "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action…". Colour-per-category coding (e.g. "Mary's sales are in red, Tom's are in blue") needs another visible distinction. A hue change combined with a 3:1 lightness contrast counts as one. Failure F73 covers links that are not evident without colour vision. — [W3C Understanding 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- Okabe–Ito (2008, Color Universal Design) has 8 colours:
  - Orange #E69F00
  - Sky Blue #56B4E9
  - Bluish Green #009E73
  - Yellow #F0E442
  - Blue #0072B2
  - Vermillion #D55E00
  - Reddish Purple #CC79A7
  - Black #000000

  — [ConceptViz reference, citing jfly.uni-koeln.de/color](https://conceptviz.app/blog/okabe-ito-palette-hex-codes-complete-reference) (secondary source; the primary is Okabe & Ito, jfly)
- Maximum class counts for ColorBrewer qualitative palettes:

  | Palette | Max classes |
  |---|---|
  | Accent | 8 |
  | Dark2 | 8 |
  | Pastel2 | 8 |
  | Set2 | 8 |
  | Pastel1 | 9 |
  | Set1 | 9 |
  | Paired | 12 |
  | Set3 | 12 |

  The minimum is 3. — [RColorBrewer docs](https://rdrr.io/pkg/RColorBrewer/man/ColorBrewer.html)

### Inferences
- Keep navigation or category colours to about 5–8, always paired with text labels or icons. Okabe–Ito's vermillion, orange, blue and sky blue overlap well with the gel and sodium vocabulary from section 4.

### Gaps
- No Tableau 10 documentation was retrieved. No perceptual study giving a hard limit on distinguishable categorical colours was retrieved.
